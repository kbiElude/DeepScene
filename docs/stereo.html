How stereo separation is done

Two cameras a little apart, an agreed plane where their pictures coincide, and limits so that nothing is uncomfortable. This page derives the maths, says where in the code each number comes from, and lists what goes wrong when one is off.

What we are after

Each eye gets a picture of the same scene from its own camera. The cameras are parallel and a separation s apart; they are not turned toward each other (toe-in), since that gives vertical parallax and a keystone that eyes do not forgive. Parallel cameras alone would put everything in front of the screen, so each picture is also shifted sideways until one chosen distance d, the convergence distance, has no parallax: what is there appears at the screen, nearer things stand out of it, farther ones lie behind it. A shifted picture from a parallel camera is an off-axis (asymmetric) frustum, and that is the whole technique.

One formula for the parallax

Everything else on this page follows from one formula. With cameras a separation s apart converging at d, a point at depth z (measured along the way the camera looks, not the straight-line distance) has, between the two pictures, a horizontal difference of

parallax(z) = s · Px · (1/z − 1/d) / 2 (as a part of the width of the picture) Px is the x scale of the projection: cot(field of view / 2) of the width. Zero at z = d, positive for nearer, negative for farther. It is linear in 1/z, not in z: a thing that is twice as near has twice the parallax, however near it is already.

Two paths of the code produce it, because Direct3D has two kinds of programs: ones whose geometry goes through matrices that we can change, and ones that hand over vertices already in pixels.

With matrices: the view and the projection

Most programs set a view matrix and a projection matrix and then draw model-space vertices. We change those two matrices and nothing else; the program's vertices, shaders and draw calls are untouched. Direct3D uses row vectors (v · M), and the recipe holds two rules, one for each matrix.

1. Move the camera: eye_offset on the view matrix

Moving a camera to the left by h = s/2 moves what is seen to the right, so the left eye's view matrix is the application's with a translation of +h along x multiplied in (and −h for the right eye):

Veye = V · T(±s/2, 0, 0) (+ for the left eye) RecipeApplier::make_eye_offset_matrix builds the translation; apply_rule multiplies it in. The order (post: V · T, or pre) is the recipe's, since it depends on what the program's matrix means.

2. Shift the picture: eye_convergence on the projection matrix

The moved camera alone has its parallel axis, so the two pictures differ by s·Px/z at every depth. We shear the projection so that the difference is zero at d. After the view, a point has xe = x + h (left eye). In the clip space of a Direct3D projection w = z, so

clipx = P00 · xe + P20 · z clipw = z xndc = P00 · (x + h) / z + P20 The element P20 (row 2, column 0: what the depth adds to x) turns into a constant offset of the picture, independent of depth.

Choose it so that a point straight ahead at depth d lands at zero: P00·h/d + P20 = 0. Then, for the left eye,

P20 −= h · P00 / d xndc = P00 · x / z + P00 · h · (1/z − 1/d) The right eye is the same with h → −h. The two pictures differ by 2·P00·h·(1/z − 1/d) in units where the picture is 2 wide, which is the formula above. The rule only applies to a perspective projection: an orthographic one has no depth to converge at.

In the code: RecipeApplier::apply_rule (libs/analysis/recipe), which MatrixSlots calls from StereoCore::bind_matrices (libs/stereo/core) when a scene draw is about to be made. The program's own calls that set the two matrices are not executed; the converter keeps them and puts the matrices of the eye in the device before each draw (see Making the second picture).

Without matrices: vertices in pixels

Some programs (the Direct3D 7 demo Kasparov is one) draw only pre-transformed vertices: a position in pixels and the reciprocal of the depth (rhw). No matrix moves them, so the two matrix rules have nothing to change. There is also no projection to read the angle of view from, and no camera to measure the depth of surfaces in. The recipe says them: a screen_parallax section with the convergence distance and the field of view.

The same formula, in pixels, with the shift applied directly to the vertex x:

shift(left eye) = (W / 2) / tan(fov / 2) · (s / 2) · (rhw − 1/d) pixels, positive to the right shift(right eye) = −shift(left eye) Stereo::D3D7::ScreenParallax::get_shift. W is the picture width in pixels. Because the vertex already carries rhw = 1/z, the shift is a single multiply-add per vertex.

Three guards are specific to this path, since the vertices belong to polygons that must stay whole:

  • Edges are held. A vertex within half a percent of the width of the left edge is not moved to the right, nor one at the right edge to the left. Ground that reaches the side of the picture then still does, and no gap of the colour behind it opens there. The polygon's edge is slanted a little; that is the price.
  • A draw that covers the whole view is never moved (a flash, a fade): its two pictures apart would leave a strip of the screen bare. The recipe knows such draws by key, but a key can be shared by a piece of sky that is drawn as a full-screen quad, so the converter also checks that the draw's own vertices reach both sides of the view.
  • A draw into a texture is moved like any other, so that the texture is the picture of the eye. The quad that draws the texture to the screen is flat, and is left where it is.

The decisions about which draw is moved how are in ScreenParallaxPlanner (libs/stereo/core), which the replay engine and the live converter both use; the bytes are moved by ScreenParallax (libs/stereo/d3d7).

How far apart the eyes are

The separation is not a fixed number of the program's units: a corridor and a hillside would need very different ones. It follows the distance at which the eyes converge, by the rule of thumb that the cameras of a stereo shot are a thirtieth of that distance apart, and it is scaled by what the person asks and by the person's eyes:

  • Depth strength is the player's Depth choice: Reduced (0.65), Natural (1, the default) or More (1.25). It can change while the program runs.
  • IPD scale is the person's distance between the eyes against the 64 mm that the one thirtieth is meant for. The presenter reads the distance between the two views OpenXR gives each frame, smooths it, divides by 64 mm, keeps it between 0.7 and 1.4 and says it through the frame channel when it changes by half a percent. A narrow or wide face is followed with no control.
  • The ceiling is what the eyes can bear at the nearest surface of the scene (see The comfort limit). No strength lifts it: a strength above 1 is capped by it, and one below 1 brings it down with it.
  • With no strength set (an offline replay with --eye-separation, or a test), the separation is the number that was set and nothing is scaled.

Where the eyes converge

The convergence distance is a property of the scene, so the analysis measures it rather than asking anyone. For every pass it counts how deep what it draws is, and keeps the count as a depth histogram: a row of logarithmic bins (eight to each doubling of the distance), each holding how many vertices were drawn in that range. Depth is where the origin of the object is along the way the camera looks (the translation of its model matrix, through the view matrix); it is only worked out for a perspective camera.

  • Per scene. The baker merges the histograms of the passes the recipe affects, for each frame shape, and takes the median by vertices: half of what is drawn is nearer. That is the shape's convergence distance (scene_convergence in the recipe).
  • Per draw signature. The same median for the passes that draw a signature (convergence), for the draws whose scene is not known yet.
  • At play time the converter does not jump between scenes: the distance eases toward the shape of the last frame by 5% of the way each frame, along the logarithm (a depth is felt in proportion to itself), and snaps when it is within a thousandth. The first one is taken at once. The one the player sets, if any, wins over the scene's, which wins over the draw's.

The comfort limit

Parallax grows without bound for something near the camera, and a wall at arm's length with the natural separation would be painful. The recipe's comfort section says how much is acceptable, and the baker turns it into a ceiling of the separation for each scene:

  • max_near_parallax: the most that the pictures of the eyes may be apart at the nearest surface of a scene, as a part of the width of the picture (0.03 is three in a hundred).
  • near_fraction: which part of the surfaces counts as the nearest (0.001: the nearest thousandth by vertices). A scene is rarely near everywhere, and what is very near is often a few large polygons, so it is small.

The nearest depth comes from a second histogram, of the surfaces of the draws (up to 64 vertices sampled from each draw, carried into camera space), because the origin of a large mesh can be far away while its walls are close. The ceiling is the separation at which the disparity formula gives exactly max_near_parallax for that nearest depth znear:

smax = 2 · max_near_parallax / ( Px · (1/znear − 1/d) ) only if znear < d RecipeBake bakes it as scene_max_separation per frame shape; the converter eases toward it like toward the distance, and lifts it at once for a scene that has none.

What is not moved, and what is pushed

A draw the recipe leaves alone (an interface: text, a menu) is drawn once, which means with zero parallax: at the screen. That is correct for text, but a scene that comes out in front of it makes the interface seem to sink into the scene. The recipe's comfort.overlay_parallax says how far toward the viewer such draws may be pushed, as a part of the width of the picture (0.04 is four in a hundred), and the VR player pushes by it unless told not to.

  • With matrices the push is a constant added to the same shear element: P20 += ±o (plus for the left eye), so the two pictures of the draw are o of the width apart.
  • In pixels every vertex of the draw is moved by the same o · W / 2 pixels (right for the left eye), whatever its depth.
  • Only draws made to what the program presents are pushed. A draw into a texture is left alone, since what it makes is used elsewhere. A draw that covers the whole view is not pushed either, for the strip it would leave bare.

Making the second picture

The matrices say what each eye should see; something still has to draw it twice. In the running program the converter does it by twins: every render target, depth buffer and texture the program renders to gets a copy when the program makes it, and a draw (or a clear, or a copy) is made again with the twins in place of what the program had set. The program's own calls make one eye (the left, for Direct3D 8) and the converter repeats them for the other.

For Direct3D 7 with vertices in pixels the vertices have to be copied to be moved, so a moved draw is made by the renderer for both eyes (the right first, then the left, each with its own copy of the vertices), and a draw that is not moved is made as the program made it. In an offline replay there is no running program to repeat calls for: the engine executes the whole frame twice, left then right, and puts the state of the device back between the two.

The converter also renders larger than the program asks. The program's back buffer and render targets are made a scale larger, so that the picture fills the headset's native resolution; the scale is the largest that fits an eye with the program's own proportions, never below 1 or above 4 (ResolutionPolicy, Live conversion).

What is not covered

  • A program that passes its matrices to a vertex shader as constants (SetVertexShaderConstant) has a camera that the converter cannot move: no rule can name it yet.
  • A pre-transformed vertex has no real depth, only rhw; the parallax of such a scene is as good as the field of view and convergence distance the recipe was given.
  • The separation is a function of the convergence distance of the scene, not of what the viewer looks at. There is no eye tracking.
  • Vertical parallax and toe-in are deliberately absent.
