How stereo separation is done

Two cameras a little apart, an agreed plane where their pictures coincide, and limits so that nothing is uncomfortable. This page derives the maths, says where in the code each number comes from, and lists what goes wrong when one is off.

What we are after

A stereo picture is two ordinary pictures of one scene, one for each eye. The brain compares them: a thing that sits at a different place in the two pictures is judged to be at a different distance. That horizontal difference between the two pictures is the parallax, and producing the right parallax for every part of the scene is the whole job. The rest of this page is about choosing it.

Two cameras, side by side

The two pictures come from two cameras a distance s apart, the separation, like a pair of eyes. The cameras look the same way: they are parallel. They are not turned toward each other (toe-in), because a turned camera sees the scene at a slant, which makes the two pictures differ vertically as well, and eyes cannot fuse that.

Choosing where the scene meets the screen

Two parallel cameras alone leave a problem. A thing at distance z is seen s apart in the two pictures, and the nearer it is the more. Only something infinitely far has no difference. Seen on a screen, everything would then float in front of it, and nothing would lie at the screen itself.

The fix is to slide each picture sideways by a fixed amount, so that one chosen distance d ends up with no difference. That distance is the convergence distance. A thing at d appears at the screen, a nearer thing in front of it, and a farther thing behind it.

Why the frustums are lopsided

Both cameras have exactly the same view direction and differ only in position. The easiest way to see what the slide does is to think of the screen as a window that hangs in the world at distance d, centred between the two cameras. Each camera sees the scene through that same window, and the window is what defines its picture.

The cameras are not in the same place, so neither is in front of the centre of the window: the left camera is half the separation to the left of it, the right camera as far to the right. A camera's frustum is the pyramid from the camera through the edges of its window. An ordinary camera has its window centred on its view direction, so its pyramid is symmetric. Here the window is off-centre for each camera, so the pyramid is off-axis (asymmetric): its left and right sides are at different angles.

This is the same as sliding the picture. Render the usual symmetric picture from the camera's position, but wider, and keep an off-centre part of it: you get exactly what the off-axis frustum renders directly, without drawing the part that is thrown away.

Both cameras look through the same window, so a thing at d lands at the same place in both pictures. That is the no-parallax condition we wanted.

One formula for the parallax

Everything else on this page follows from one formula. With cameras a separation s apart converging at d, a point at depth z (measured along the way the camera looks, not the straight-line distance) has, between the two pictures, a horizontal difference of

parallax(z) = s · Px · (1/z − 1/d) / 2 (as a part of the width of the picture) Px is the x scale of the projection, cot(horizontal field of view / 2). Zero at z = d, positive for nearer, negative for farther. It is linear in 1/z, not in z: a thing that is twice as near has twice the parallax, however near it is already.

Two paths of the code produce it, because programs come in two kinds: ones whose geometry goes through matrices that we can change (Direct3D 8, OpenGL), and ones that hand over vertices already in pixels (Direct3D 7, and the XYZRHW vertices that a Direct3D 8 program gives in a DrawPrimitiveUP or DrawIndexedPrimitiveUP call).

With matrices: the view and the projection

Most programs set a view matrix and a projection matrix and then draw model-space vertices. We change those two matrices and nothing else; the program's vertices, shaders and draw calls are untouched. Direct3D uses row vectors (v · M), and the recipe holds two MatrixRules, one for each matrix.

1. Move the camera: eye_offset on the view matrix

Moving a camera to the left by h = s/2 moves what is seen to the right, so the left eye's view matrix is the application's with a translation of +h along x multiplied in (and −h for the right eye):

Veye = V · T(±s/2, 0, 0) (+ for the left eye) RecipeApplier::make_eye_offset_matrix builds the translation; apply_rule multiplies it in. The order (post: V · T, or pre) is the recipe's, since it depends on what the program's matrix means.

2. Shift the picture: eye_convergence on the projection matrix

The moved camera alone has its parallel axis, so the two pictures differ by s·P00/z at every depth (in units where the picture is 2 wide). We shear the projection so that the difference is zero at d. After the view, a point has xe = x + h (left eye). In the clip space of a Direct3D projection w = z, so

clipx = P00 · xe + P20 · z clipw = z xndc = P00 · (x + h) / z + P20 The element P20 (row 2, column 0: what the depth adds to x) turns into a constant offset of the picture, independent of depth.

Choose it so that a point straight ahead at depth d lands at zero: P00·h/d + P20 = 0. Then, for the left eye,

P20 −= h · P00 / d xndc = P00 · x / z + P00 · h · (1/z − 1/d) The right eye is the same with h → −h. The two pictures differ by 2·P00·h·(1/z − 1/d) in units where the picture is 2 wide, which is s·Px·(1/z − 1/d) / 2 of its width: the formula above. The rule only applies to a perspective projection: an orthographic one has no depth to converge at.

In the code: RecipeApplier::apply_rule (libs/analysis/recipe), which RecipeStereoPlanner::bind_matrices reaches through MatrixSlots (libs/stereo/core) when a scene draw is about to be made. The program's own calls that set the two matrices are not executed; the converter keeps them and puts the matrices of the eye in the device before each draw (see Making the second picture).

Without matrices: vertices in pixels

Some programs (Direct3D 7 ones), or some draws of a Direct3D 8 program, use pre-transformed vertices: a position in pixels and the reciprocal of the depth (rhw). No matrix moves them, so the two matrix rules have nothing to change, and there is no projection to read the angle of view from. The recipe's screen_parallax section gives the field of view, and a convergence distance for the draws whose scene has none.

The same formula, in pixels, with the shift applied directly to the vertex x:

shift(left eye) = (W / 2) / tan(fov / 2) · (s / 2) · (rhw − 1/d) pixels, positive to the right shift(right eye) = −shift(left eye) Stereo::Core::ScreenSpaceVertexShifter::get_shift. W is the picture width in pixels, fov the horizontal field of view of screen_parallax. Because the vertex already carries rhw = 1/z, the shift is a single multiply-add per vertex.

How far apart the eyes are

The separation is not a fixed number of the program's units: a corridor and a hillside would need very different ones. It follows the distance at which the eyes converge, by the rule of thumb that the cameras of a stereo shot are a thirtieth of that distance apart, and it is scaled by what the person asks and by the person's eyes:

Where the eyes converge

The convergence distance is a property of the scene, so the analysis measures it instead of asking anyone. For every pass it counts how deep what the pass draws is, and keeps the count as a depth histogram: a row of logarithmic bins (eight to each doubling of the distance), each holding the number of vertices drawn in that range.

Depth is where the origin of the object is along the way the camera looks (the translation of its model matrix, through the view matrix). It is only worked out for a perspective camera. For a draw whose vertices are in pixels it is the median of 1/rhw over its vertices.

That gives a distance per scene. Where it is used depends on what is known when a draw is made:

BalancedWith comfort.balance_convergence and both parallax limits, the distance goes where the nearest surfaces have all of the near limit and the farthest all of the far one (below).A scene with a sky behind a near object, which the median would make flat. A distance that scene_overrides gives for a scene is kept.

The balanced distance solves 1/d = 1/znear − (1/znear − 1/zfar) · near / (near + far), where near and far are the two parallax limits. In 1/z it splits the span between the farthest and the nearest surface in the ratio of the limits.

Easing

The distance eases toward the target of the last frame along the logarithm, since a depth is felt in proportion to itself: 5% of the way in each frame. Two comfort options of the recipe change that, and both are off unless it asks for them: ease_by_time makes the step depend on the time the frame took, and snap_at_cuts takes a cut at once.

The comfort limit

Parallax grows without bound for something near the camera, and a wall at arm's length with the natural separation would be painful. The recipe's comfort section says how much is acceptable, and the baker turns it into a ceiling of the separation for each scene.

FieldMeaning
max_near_parallaxThe most that the pictures of the eyes may be apart at the nearest surface of a scene, as a part of the width of the picture (0.03 is three in a hundred).
near_fractionWhich part of the surfaces counts as the nearest, and as the farthest (0.001, the default: the nearest thousandth by vertices). A scene is rarely near everywhere, and what is very near is often a few large polygons, so it is small.
max_far_parallaxThe same for the farthest surface, which is beyond the convergence distance: the eyes would have to turn away from each other if the pictures were too far apart there. 0 is no limit, as it is for the near one.
scene_overridesThe same numbers (and a distance or a ceiling) for single scenes, by their shape keys.

The nearest depth is the nearer of two estimates: the origins of the draws (the histogram above) and their surfaces, a second histogram of up to 64 vertices sampled from each draw and carried into camera space. The surfaces count because the origin of a large mesh can be far away while its walls are close.

The ceiling is the separation at which the disparity formula gives exactly max_near_parallax for that nearest depth znear:

smax = 2 · max_near_parallax / ( Px · (1/znear − 1/d) ) only if znear < d RecipeApplier::get_comfort_limits works it out (for the farthest surface the same with 1/d − 1/zfar, and the smaller of the two is the ceiling); RecipeBake bakes it as scene_max_separation per frame shape. The converter eases toward it as toward the distance, and lifts it at once for a known scene that has none; a frame of a scene the recipe does not know keeps the ceiling of the one before.

Draws attached to the camera

A weapon or a sky centred on the viewer is nearer than everything else, and would take nearly all of the separation. A draw whose origin is nearer the camera than comfort.camera_attached_depth (a part of the convergence distance; 0 is none) is made as it is for both eyes. The OpenGL converter applies this; the Direct3D ones do not.

FieldMeaning
camera_attached_depthThe part of the convergence distance under which an origin counts as attached to the camera.
eye_space_cover_keysDraws (by signature and size) whose model-view is the identity because the application has put their vertices in the space of the eye. An identity has an origin of 0 whatever the vertices are, so these are kept out of the rule above and given the parallax of the depth of their vertices.

The decision is RecipeStereoPlanner::is_last_draw_attached_to_camera(origin_depth). The OpenGL adapter gives it how far in front of the camera the origin is, from the z translation of the model-view matrix.

The other comfort fields are explained where they act: balance_convergence and measure_depth in Where the eyes converge; overlay_parallax, part_parallax and automatic_layers in What is not moved, and what is pushed.

What is not moved, and what is pushed

A draw the recipe leaves alone (an interface: text, a menu) is drawn the same in both pictures, which means with zero parallax: at the screen. That is right for text, but a scene that comes out in front of it makes the interface seem to sink into the scene. The fix is to push such draws toward the viewer by a little parallax of their own.

Which draws are pushed

The push is the same amount, overlay_parallax, as a part of the width of the picture (0.04 is four in a hundred). Which draws get it is one rule in RecipeApplier::decide_draw(), the same for every API and for the replay:

A draw is pushed ifNote
the recipe lists it in pushedLeft alone or affected. An affected one is pushed too in OpenGL, so that text and a logo stand off the layer they are drawn on. A fade, or a blur or glow that works on a copy of the picture, is not listed and so stays where it is.
the recipe sets comfort.push_unaffected and the rules leave it aloneFor an application whose left-alone draws are all an overlay (an interface). Direct3D recipes set it; the OpenGL recipe lists draws instead.

The switch of the person (the VR player's, or APISTEREO_PUSH_OVERLAY) turns the push off for every API. Direct3D adds two limits: a draw into a texture is not pushed, because what it makes is used elsewhere, and neither is a draw that covers the view, which would leave a strip bare. In Direct3D a listed draw that the rules affect gets no push.

How a push is made

CaseThe push
With matricesA constant added to the same shear element: P20 += ±o (plus for the left eye), so the two pictures of the draw are o of the width apart.
In pixelsEvery vertex of the draw is moved by the same o · W / 2 pixels (right for the left eye), whatever its depth.
OpenGLThe viewport is moved sideways around the draw, by half of the parallax for each eye, whatever the projection of the draw is (below).

In OpenGL: the viewport moves

  • The draw is made once for each eye with its viewport shifted, from its glBegin through the whole of its calls. GLEyeRenderer moves the viewport and puts it back.
  • The shift is RecipeStereoPlanner::plan_push_shift_with_layer(), from the effective separation of the scene and the layer of the draw (see below). A part uses plan_part_shift().
  • A call that makes several draws (a display list) is pushed only if all of them are.
  • The draw rule baked for a pushed signature carries "pushed": true.

Two limits on a push

A draw that reaches from one side of the picture to the other (a 2D bar or a scroller, named in baked.side_reaching_draws) would leave bare the side that it moves away from. Its viewport is made as much wider as the shift on each side, so the edge that the shift would expose stays where it was and the other goes past the edge of the view (PushShiftPlanner::make_viewport, the same for the converter and the replay).

The push is also bounded by the comfort limit. The parallax is overlay_parallax, but no more than what is left of max_near_parallax after the layer of the draw.

Making the second picture

The matrices say what each eye should see; something still has to draw it twice. How that is done depends on the API, but the parts are the same: the planner (RecipeStereoPlanner) decides, an adapter in the converter carries it out.

Twins: one set of images for each eye

Every render target, depth buffer and texture the program renders to gets a copy, its twin, when the program makes it. A draw (or a clear, or a copy) is made again with the twins in place of what the program had set.

  • The program's own calls make one eye: the left, for Direct3D 8.
  • The converter repeats them on the twins for the other eye.
  • The exception is a draw of vertices in pixels that is moved: it is made for both eyes from copies.

OpenGLThe camera is the model-view matrix, so the converter makes every draw itself, for both eyes, and the program's own call is not made. Each eye gets an eye matrix in front of the matrix the draw has (below, and Live conversion).
Offline replayThere is no running program to repeat calls for: the engine executes the whole frame twice, left then right, and puts the state of the device back between the two.
Mdraw = Eeye · Mprogram Eeye: x → x + h + (h / d) · z, h = +s/2 for the left eye, −s/2 for the right RecipeStereoPlanner::make_eye_shift_matrix: the translation h is element 12 of the column-major matrix, the shear h/d element 8. In the camera space of OpenGL the scene lies along −z, so a point at z = −d is shifted by h − h = 0: what is at the convergence distance stays put. With no convergence distance only the translation is made.

Rendering larger

The converter also renders larger than the program asks, so that the picture fills the headset's native resolution. The program's back buffer and render targets are made a scale larger. The scale is the least of three limits (what fits an eye with the program's own proportions, what the frame channel holds, what a surface of the API can be), never below 1 or above 4 (RenderScalePlanner, ResolutionPolicy; Live conversion).

What is not covered

  • A program that passes its matrices to a vertex shader as constants (SetVertexShaderConstant) has a camera that the converter cannot move: no rule can name it.
  • A pre-transformed vertex has no real depth, only rhw; the parallax of such a scene is as good as the field of view and convergence distance the recipe was given.
  • The separation is a function of the convergence distance of the scene, not of what the viewer looks at. There is no eye tracking.
  • Vertical parallax and toe-in are deliberately absent.
