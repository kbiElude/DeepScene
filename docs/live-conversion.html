Live conversion

The converter lives inside the program it converts. It watches each call, decides how a draw is treated, renders the second eye, and hands both images to another process. This page covers its structure, the resolution, the pacing, the adapters of Direct3D 7, Direct3D 8 and OpenGL, the frame channel and the log.

Inside the program

Decisions and adapters

The converter is made of an adapter, which knows the API of the application, and of decisions that do not. RecipeStereoPlanner, ConvergenceModel, MatrixSlots, the frame channel, the presenter and the VR player have no API in them: an adapter tells RecipeStereoPlanner what the application does (it sets a matrix, it draws) and does what it answers.

The pieces

PieceJob
LiveStereoConverterRegisters the callbacks and orders the work of each call: feed the analysis, keep matrices, classify and render draws, take the settings the VR player changed. A call that nothing reads gets no callback: the OpenGL calls that set a color, a normal or a texture coordinate (unless the recipe serves pixel reads from one picture, which drops the picture on any call), and the callback after a vertex call, which has no result and is analysed before it is made.
LiveCallAnalyzerGives the analysis each call as a dump would, for Direct3D 7, Direct3D 8 and OpenGL alike; keeps the calls that wait for results in per-thread pools, which hand their vectors back to the next call.
RecipeStereoPlannerThe decisions: the class of a draw from the recipe and the view matrix, unknown draws and frames. Owns ConvergenceModel (where the eyes converge and how far apart they are) and MatrixSlots (the matrices a rule is about, and which to put in before a draw).
The adapter for an APIRenders the second eye, scales the resolution, shows the program in a window or hides it, and exports the frames. The classes are listed below.
PacedFramePublisher, SessionLogWhat the adapters share: pass the presented frame on to the channel (at most one in 8 ms) and wait for the headset to want the next (HostMemoryExporterBase::pace(), in the base of the three exporters, does the waiting and the hold to a rate); one log line per event.
InvisibleWindowManager, EnvironmentVariableReaderAlso shared: make the windows of the program invisible and keep them so; read the APISTEREO_* switches (see the switches).

Details of the converter and the call analyzer

  • LiveStereoConverter keeps what it knows of the running program (RecipeStereoPlanner, the call analyzer, the renderers, the counters) in one struct behind one lock (Utils::Parallel::SrwMutex), and no other lock is taken while it is held.
  • Callback times are sampled, one call in 61.
  • LiveCallAnalyzer takes the data chunks of a call out of the interceptor in one go, into vectors from a pool (APIInterceptor::ChunkPool) which the next call gets back, so that a chunk is not an allocation.
  • The calls it makes carry no timestamp (timestamp_qpc is 0): nothing in the converter looks at when a call was made.

What a converter cannot know

A converter knows less when a draw is issued than a replay does. The pass is not known at that point, so a draw is told by its signature and the view matrix, and a rule about a pass or a frame shape cannot be followed. Where a program needs more than that, the converter ends it at once, with a message, instead of producing wrong images (RecipeStereoPlanner::require_fast_path, require_supported). The same goes for a recipe that is not baked.

AdapterEnds the program when it
Direct3D 8
  • locks a render target, or resets the device;
  • renders or draws to a surface the converter did not see being made, or renders to a cube or volume texture;
  • lets its window decide the size of the back buffer (0 × 0);
  • draws vertices positioned in pixels (D3DFVF_XYZRHW, as a HUD or text uses) from a vertex buffer. The ones the call carries (DrawPrimitiveUP, DrawIndexedPrimitiveUP) are drawn for both eyes by the renderer (see Vertices in pixels);
  • does what depends on the size of what it renders to, while it is rendered larger or in another place of the image (ResolutionHazardGate); a program that sets a vertex format in pixels is one of these, because its vertices are not scaled by the scaler of Direct3D 8.
Direct3D 7Only when it draws vertices in pixels while bars move its picture (see Resolution per eye). A call it cannot repeat for the right eye is counted and told in the log, and the right eye shows wrong content there.
OpenGL
  • does what depends on the size of what it renders to, while it is rendered larger or in another place of the image (see Refused calls);
  • would need a texture or framebuffer larger than the driver makes, or an eye image larger than the channel passes on.

Hiding the program

When the images are shown elsewhere, the program must not also take the screen. The adapters differ in when they make it render to an invisible window.

AdapterWhen the program is hiddenHow
Direct3D 8Always.D3D8WindowedMode changes the parameters of the device: a window, the desktop format, one back buffer, a swap effect that a window supports.
Direct3D 7When the channel has an eye size (see below).D3D7WindowedMode, described under Vertices in pixels.
OpenGLWhen the channel has an eye size.The program renders to the framebuffers of GLEyeTargets and never to its window.
  • The VR player always writes an eye size to the channel before the program starts (0 × 0 without a headset and without a chosen resolution), so a Direct3D 7 or OpenGL program is hidden whenever the presenter shows it.
  • A run without the VR player has no size in the channel. The program then stays on the screen as it is, unless APISTEREO_TARGET_EYE_WIDTH and _HEIGHT are both set.
  • InvisibleWindowManager makes a window fully transparent and click-through. It is not hidden, because some programs pause while their window is hidden. It also tells the program that its window is always active, because some pause while it is not, and an invisible window is never the foreground one.
  • The manager makes the window invisible again after each frame, since a program may restyle its window.

A frame

  1. The program sets matrices and draws. The hook gives each call to the call analyzer. A draw is analysed immediately (its signature is needed before it runs, and so is whether it samples a copy of the picture, which a recipe with leave_picture_filters_unpushed asks); other calls are kept until their results are in.
  2. RecipeStereoPlanner looks the draw up in the recipe. A scene draw is made for each eye with the eye's matrices; an interface draw is made once; a skipped one is not made. The maths of the matrices, the separation and the convergence is on the Stereo page.
  3. At the end of the frame the images go to the frame channel.

How the images reach the channel

Each adapter has an exporter that copies the two eyes into the frame channel, side by side. They differ in who does the copy.

APICopy
Direct3D 8
  • The GPU copies each eye to a system-memory surface that the exporter keeps (two sets).
  • A thread of the exporter (a Utils::Parallel::JobThread) copies the rows of the left eye to the channel while the program renders the next frame. A second thread copies the right eye.
  • The program waits for the threads only when it has the next frame to hand over.
OpenGLPixel buffers filled by the graphics card; one thread turns the rows over into the channel (see OpenGL, The images).
Direct3D 7No thread. The program's own thread locks the surfaces and copies the rows into the channel, converted to B, G, R of 8 bits. The images are small.

Resolution

A program that asks for a back buffer of 800 × 600 gives a small picture in a headset. The converter therefore renders it larger, at the resolution of the headset.

Where the size comes from

  1. The headset library asks the OpenXR runtime for the recommended size of the image of an eye (xrEnumerateViewConfigurationViews); the VR player scales it by the fraction the user chose (Headset: full is 1), unless the user chose a size per eye, which is used as it is (see Resolution per eye).
  2. The VR player puts the size in the frame channel before the program starts (FrameChannel::set_target_eye_size()), within what the channel holds.
  3. When the program makes its device or window, the adapter works out the scale from the size the program asked for (ResolutionPolicy::get_fit_scale()): the largest that fits the eye with the program's own proportions, so nothing is stretched.
  4. The back buffer, and what is rendered to at its size, is made that many times larger. What the program states in pixels is adjusted to match.

A size per eye

The VR player can instead ask for a size per eye (FrameChannel::set_target_eye_size(); StereoPresenter --eye-size does the same), with or without a headset. The image of each eye has exactly that size. The program is rendered at its own proportions with the scale above, and RenderScalePlanner::choose_layout places that picture in the middle of the image. A program whose picture does not fit (it would have to be smaller than it asks, or the API cannot make the surface) is made as large as it asks, and the image is that size.

The adaptersThe back buffer or framebuffers are the frame. What the analysis sees

The analysis must see what the program asked for, not what was scaled, or the signatures of draws would depend on the scale.

  • Direct3D 8: the scaler writes the original sizes back into the device parameters that the call analyzer sees (D3D8ResolutionScaler::restore_chunk, installed with LiveCallAnalyzer::set_chunk_func).
  • Direct3D 7 and OpenGL: where a call must differ, the converter does not make the program's call but its own. The call reaches the analysis as the program made it.

Keeping the frames even

In a headset two things are felt: how late a picture is, and whether the motion is even. A frame that is shown twice with the one after it skipped is judder, and it is felt even when every frame is fast. The converter and the presenter therefore do three things:

  • they get a frame from the program to the headset with as little work on the program's own thread as they can;
  • they make the program produce one frame for each frame of the headset;
  • they hold the headset's frame for an image that is a few milliseconds late.

The way of a frame

Each frame passes four threads. None waits for the one after it, except that the program waits for the exporter's thread when it has its next frame to hand over. The figure is that of Direct3D 8 and OpenGL; with Direct3D 7 the program's own thread does the copy to the channel.

One frame for each frame of the headset

A program that renders as fast as it can, and a headset that shows frames at a fixed rate, drift against each other: a frame of the program is made that the compositor never takes, or a frame is taken twice. It is felt as judder even when nothing is slow.

With the headset, the presenter therefore tells the program when to make a frame. Each time the compositor asks for one (xrWaitFrame returns) the presenter signals the frame channel (FrameChannel::signal_pace(), a named event). The converter, after the program has presented a frame, waits for that signal (wait_for_pace()) and only then lets the program begin the next one.

PartWhat it does
The delayThe frame is not begun at the signal but a delay after it, so that its images are ready at the right moment.
Counted from the signalThe delay runs from the signal, not from the moment the program notices it. A frame that has to wait is then not longer by the delay, and one that is late goes on at once.
The timerA high-resolution timer wakes the thread about a millisecond early and a polling loop covers the rest, since a wait as coarse as a system tick would be a whole frame.
The right momentThe compositor builds its layers as soon as it asks for a frame, from the images that are ready by then. The presenter measures how far into a frame of the headset each set of images is ready (HeadsetSink::tune_pace()) and moves the delay, a tenth of the error per frame, until the images are ready at 0.3 of a frame. It depends on how long the copies take, so it cannot be worked out beforehand.
Where it cannot hurtA program is not made to wait for a presenter that has not signalled for a quarter of a second (a headset that was taken off), nor longer than 100 ms for one signal. The delay and the signal are in the header of the channel.

An image that is a few milliseconds late

The copy of a frame to the headset takes a good part of a frame and varies, so the images are sometimes ready just after the compositor asks. Without the wait, that frame shows the one before again, and the next shows the new images a frame late: a repeat and a skip for one late copy. The compositor's frame can be ended any time within its period, and it is ended in under a millisecond, so there is room to wait.

Lag and options

The lag of the picture behind what the program did is the work of the four threads plus the margin before the next request: about two frames of the headset. The program's own sound is not delayed, so the picture trails the music by that much. The margin is on purpose; a smaller one gives a shorter lag and more repeats when a copy is late.

  • No preview with the headset. The channel has room for one reader, and the preview's copy of each image costs the same memory bandwidth as the headset's. The window says so; without a headset it previews the images.
  • Pacing can be turned off (--no-pacing on the presenter), to see the difference or for a program whose timing depends on how fast it presents. The VR player always paces.

Vertices in pixels: Direct3D 7 and 8

A program of Direct3D 7 whose vertices are all in pixels is converted by the same pieces with a different renderer. The same LiveCallAnalyzer follows its calls through the analysis, RecipeStereoPlanner decides each draw from the recipe, and what is done to the vertices is decided by ScreenParallaxPlanner, which the replay uses too, with the bytes moved by Stereo::Core::ScreenSpaceVertexShifter.

Only vertices that are already in pixels (pre-transformed) are moved. A matrix that the device applies is left alone: such a draw looks the same in both eyes.

CallUsed by
RecipeStereoPlanner::make_screen_vertices()Gives an adapter a copy of the vertices moved for the eye.
D3D7EyeRendererUses it for every draw of vertices in pixels.
D3D8EyeRenderer::on_pre_draw()Uses it for DrawPrimitiveUP and DrawIndexedPrimitiveUP draws whose vertex format is XYZRHW (D3D8Frontend::is_pre_transformed_vertex_shader) and makes those draws for both eyes itself. XYZRHW vertices from a vertex buffer end the application with a message (see Stereo).

The second eye

D3D7SurfaceTwins makes a copy of every surface the program renders to (render targets, depth buffers, its back buffer) as the program makes them. For each draw or clear, D3D7EyeRenderer repeats the work on the twins.

The window

When another program shows the images, D3D7WindowedMode has the program render to an invisible window (see Hiding the program).

  • The cooperative level is that of a window, the display mode is not set, and the surface that is shown has no chain of buffers.
  • The back buffer the program gets is a surface of the converter's own, the size of the display mode times the render scale (or the size of the eye image, when one was asked for).
  • A flip shows nothing.
  • The hooks cannot change an argument of Direct3D 7 in place. The converter therefore does not make the program's call: it makes its own and writes the result where the program is to get it.

The resolution

  • The back buffer, its twin and the depth buffer are made as large as the images are shown (the same policy as Direct3D 8; surfaces are capped at 2048 on a side).
  • The viewport, the rectangles of a clear and the positions of the vertices are scaled to match. With bars around the picture, the viewport and the rectangles are moved too, and vertices in pixels are not: such a program is stopped.
  • A program that locks the whole back buffer, writes it and unlocks it (a title or an ending screen, say) has it stretched to the whole after the unlock and copied to the twin.
  • A lock to read, or of part of the surface, is counted, and the log has it (calls on the back buffer in pixels it no longer has).

The images

The back buffer is read before a flip, or before the blit of the surface the program rendered to onto the primary surface (how a program in a window shows a frame). It is converted to B, G, R of 8 bits and put into the channel, the two eyes side by side. When no screen keeps time (no program that shows the images, or one that does not say), the converter holds the program to a frame rate (see the switches).

OpenGL

A program of OpenGL (fixed function, a camera that is the model-view matrix) is converted by the same pieces with GLEyeRenderer.

Which calls draw

  • LiveCallAnalyzer follows the OpenGL and WGL calls and both SwapBuffers (the one of GDI32 ends a frame like the one of WGL).
  • It takes glCallList, glDrawArrays, glDrawElements and glEnd as the calls that draw.
  • A call can make several draws (a list), so the model builder keeps the cover key and the signature key of each draw that the last call made (get_call_draw_key_vec), and the converter asks RecipeStereoPlanner about each one.
  • A call counts as affected if any of its draws is, else as unaffected if any is, else as skipped. A list that mixes classes is counted and told in the log, since the driver makes all draws of a list in one call.

The eyes

GLEyeTargets makes two framebuffers of renderbuffers (color, and depth with stencil), plus a small one for reading the depth, and binds the one of the left eye for the application. The color renderbuffers have exactly the channels of the pixel format that the driver gave the window of the application (GetPixelFormat and DescribePixelFormat of its device context, see GLColorTargetFormats): with alpha bits the targets have alpha, without them they have none and alpha reads as 1. A pixel format that has no target of exactly its channels ends the process. The application never draws to its window, so it does not matter that the window cannot be seen.

A program that makes a framebuffer object of its own (glGenFramebuffers, glBindFramebuffer and the calls that fill one) is not followed: the front end has no handler for those calls, so the process is ended and the log names the call, whatever else the recipe says. The converter therefore never has to give the program a framebuffer of its own back, and it binds the framebuffers of the eyes without keeping track of the program's binding.

  • The functions of framebuffers are found through the wglGetProcAddress that the hooks call through, not the one the application sees. The hooked extension functions have no driver functions behind them until the application makes its context current.
  • The functions of OpenGL 1.1 that the converter calls itself are the driver's too (GLDriverFunctions, from the cached pointers of the hooks). A hook also runs the callbacks and copies the data of a call (a read of pixels) for a program that watches the application, which the converter's own calls do not need.
  • The framebuffers are made when the application makes its context current and at each SwapBuffers, so they follow a window that changes size.

A draw

A draw of glDrawArrays, glDrawElements or glCallList is made by the converter for both eyes, and the call of the application is not made. The right eye is drawn first.

Class of the drawWhat the converter does
AffectedEach eye gets the matrix of the eye (RecipeStereoPlanner::make_eye_shift_matrix(): half the separation to the side and a shear by the depth, so that what is at the distance of convergence stays put) in front of the model-view matrix the draw has, which is read and put back.
UnaffectedMade for both eyes as it is.
SkippedNot made.
Pushed (see Stereo)Moved by moving the origin of its viewport (PushShiftPlanner::make_viewport; a side-reaching overlay is widened by the shift on each side). The shift is in RecipeStereoPlanner::plan_push_shift_with_layer().
  • Any camera is a model-view matrix, so the anchor of the replay (the call that begins the camera) is not needed live.
  • The indices of glDrawElements are the chunk the call analyzer took out of the interceptor.

Pushed draws

  • For an affected pushed draw the renderer reads the projection and the model-view and adds the parallax of its depth (RecipeStereoPlanner::get_eye_parallax()). A projection that is not perspective (DrawMeasurer::is_perspective_projection) says nothing of depth.
  • A part that protrudes from an object (the recipe says which draws are parts) is shifted for each eye to where the eyes would have put it, plus the push, limited to the parallax the scene allows (RecipeStereoPlanner::plan_part_shift()).

A block of glBegin to glEnd

Whether a block is affected is known at glEnd, after its vertices were given. The block is therefore compiled to a display list that the converter owns, and the list is called for each eye like a draw. A recipe can have the block recorded instead (see Recorded blocks).

Recorded blocks

The display list of the block is the default. A recipe can ask for the block to be recorded instead, and then no display list is made: the converter keeps the calls of the block in a buffer of its own, and at glEnd it makes them for each eye with the driver's own functions. A driver compiles a display list at a cost that is larger than the whole analysis of the block when an application gives thousands of blocks a frame. Making the calls themselves costs about what the application pays for the block natively.

WhatHow it is made
The switchThe recipe field record_immediate_mode_blocks (off unless it says true). APISTEREO_RECORD_IMMEDIATE_MODE_BLOCKS decides it for a run: 1 on, 0 off, whatever the recipe says. A recipe with cache_pixel_reads never records, because that needs a callback on every call, and the log says so.
What is recordedGLBlockRecorder holds glVertex2f, glVertex3f, glColor3f, glColor4f, glNormal3f and glTexCoord2f, and their v forms. Those calls are not made at the time: the hook gets should_pass_through false, as for the glBegin and glEnd of the block.
At glEndThe block is made for each eye as a recorded block (GLBlockRecorder::play(), inside GLEyeRenderer::play_eyes()), with the class and the eye matrix that a block of a display list gets.
A call the recorder does not holdAny other call in the block, or a type it does not hold (glVertex3d, glColor4ub, glMaterial, glCallList, glEdgeFlag), hands the block to a display list: the calls so far are compiled into one (GLEyeRenderer::fall_back_to_list()) and the rest of the block follows. The first of each kind is told in the log. Calls that do not belong to OpenGL (the window functions) are ignored.
The analysisThe calls that set an attribute, and the vertex calls that the recorder holds, have a callback of their own (LiveStereoConverter::on_attribute_callback) and are not analysed one by one. The analysis needs only how many vertices the block has: the renderer counts them, and the front end is given the count just before it analyses the glEnd (LiveCallAnalyzer::add_block_vertices()). This is allowed only when no positions are kept, which is the case for a live converter.
The logOne line at the start says that the blocks are recorded and where that was decided. The counts of the log line (every 600 frames) give the blocks made from a recording and how many of them were handed to a display list.
What was checkedA program that draws the same scene from the frame number alone, with blocks of every kind and some that cannot be recorded, gives the same eye images with and without the recording, up to the noise between two runs of one setting (some hundred pixels at the edges of the shapes). The tests cover the recipe field and that the front end finds the same with the vertices counted and not given.

The model-view and projection matrices of a draw and the matrix mode come from the analysis, which follows the matrix calls of the application; the driver is not asked for them. The first 64 draws and then one in 256 are also checked against the driver. The first difference makes the converter ask the driver from then on and says so in the log. Each eye gets one matrix, the eye matrix times the draw's own, loaded at once.

The right eye once for a run

Drawing both eyes at each block binds the framebuffer of the right eye and then the one of the left eye again for every block. The graphics driver pays for a change of render target when the next draw arrives, so a program that gives thousands of blocks a frame spends most of its time in those changes, not in the draws. A recipe can have the right eye drawn once for a whole run of the program's calls instead.

WhatHow it is made
The switchThe recipe field render_left_then_right_eye (off unless it says true). APISTEREO_RENDER_LEFT_THEN_RIGHT_EYE decides it for a run: 1 on, 0 off, whatever the recipe says. It needs the blocks to be recorded (Recorded blocks): the log says so if they are not, and a recipe with cache_pixel_reads never has it.
The left eyeLive. The calls of the program reach the driver as it makes them, and each block is drawn for the left eye at its glEnd. The framebuffer of the left eye stays bound.
The right eyeKept (GLRunRecorder): each block with the matrices, the twin texture and the viewport that the right eye needs, and the state calls between the blocks. The first thing a run keeps makes the driver save the state (glPushAttrib with all groups).
When the run is playedGLEyeRenderer::flush_run() binds the framebuffer of the right eye, restores the saved state, makes the kept items in order, loads the matrices and the matrix mode that the left eye has, and binds the left eye again. The calls are the same ones applied to the same start state, so the state at the end is the one the left eye has.
Which calls are keptGLRunCallClassifier decides, for every call, one of: kept (the state calls glEnable, glDisable, glBindTexture, glBlendFunc, glColorMask, glCullFace, glDepthFunc, glDepthMask, glFrontFace, glStencilFunc, glStencilOp and glTexEnvi, and the colors, normals and texture coordinates set between blocks); not kept because each draw carries its matrices (the matrix calls, while the matrix mode is the model-view or the projection); needing nothing (glBegin, glEnd, other APIs); answered from the left eye (every glGet and glIs call, which do not end a run).
What plays the run firstEvery other call of OpenGL, the ones that make or change a context (wglMakeCurrent) and the end of the frame. That includes the calls that change what the right eye would see (a texture upload or parameter, a delete, a display list, a clear, a copy to a texture, the viewport and scissor, an attribute push or pop, glCallList, the array draws) and any call the converter does not know. A block that cannot be kept (the matrices are not tracked) is drawn for both eyes after the run is played.
The matricesEach draw carries the model-view matrix the right eye loads (the eye matrix times the draw's own for an affected draw) and the projection, both from the matrices that the analysis tracks. A matrix call changes nothing for the right eye, so it is not kept; a change of the texture matrix plays the run first.
The checkEvery 32nd run, the converter compares eight capabilities (cull face, lighting, fog, depth test, stencil test, alpha test, blend, texture 2D) after the replay with the ones the left eye left. A difference is counted and told in the log.
The logOne line at the start says that it is on and where that was decided. The counts of the log line (every 600 frames) give the runs, the blocks and the state calls made for the right eye, and the differences found by the check.
When not to use itFor a program whose right eye depends on the order of calls that the table above cannot hold, or that is fast enough as it is. It is off unless the recipe asks. An application that binds a framebuffer object is not followed (the front end ends it), so it does not reach this code.
What was checkedA program that draws the same scenes from the frame number alone (blocks of every kind, state between the blocks, queries between blocks, a copy to a texture and a draw with that texture, a texture upload in a run, a display list, a texture matrix, an attribute push, a clear in the middle of a frame and an overlay with another projection) gives the same eye images with the right eye deferred as without, and as with display lists. Turning off the playing of the run before a texture upload and a clear makes thousands of pixels of the images differ. The tests cover the classification of every OpenGL function and the recipe field.

Clears and textures

  • A glClear is made for the right eye as well.
  • What the application renders to a texture (glCopyTexImage2D, glCopyTexSubImage2D) is copied for the right eye, from its framebuffer, to a twin of the texture. The twin is made when the first copy is seen, with the parameters and the size of the level.
  • The draws of the right eye bind the twin of a texture bound to the active unit, and a glTexParameter call is made to the twin too.
  • A call that cannot be made for the right eye is counted and told in the log, once (see Not followed).

The images

  • At SwapBuffers GLFrameReader starts reading both eyes from the framebuffers into pixel buffers (as B, G, R, A, the order of the channel). The graphics card fills them while the application goes on.
  • It gives the images of the frame before, from the buffers of that frame, which are mapped. With no pixel buffers the images are read at once.
  • There are two sets of buffers. A set is not used again while what was given from it is held (the images carry a token).
  • GLHostMemoryExporter has a thread that turns the rows over (OpenGL has the bottom row first) into the channel, side by side. A frame that is given while the thread is at the one before is left out.
  • Without a program that shows the images the window shows the left eye (a blit to the back buffer).
  • APISTEREO_DUMP_FRAME, APISTEREO_DUMP_EVERY and APISTEREO_DUMP_FIRST_FRAME write the images of the eyes to a file, as for Direct3D 7. APISTEREO_DUMP_SCENES writes scene images (see the switches).

The size

The framebuffers are made as large as the render scale says, or as the eye image when one was asked for (see Resolution). What the application states in pixels is made to match by GLResolutionScaler. The call of the application is not made, and the analysis still sees it as it was made.

cache_pixel_reads

An application that samples the picture on a grid reads one pixel more than a thousand times in a row, and each read waits for the graphics card.

An application that has set the way pixels are packed, or a buffer for them, is read as it is.

Refused calls

  • Any other read of pixels (more than one pixel, the depth, floats, other colours), glGetTexImage, or drawing pixels at places in pixels (glDrawPixels, glBitmap, glCopyPixels, glPixelZoom).
  • Asking for the viewport, the scissor box, the width of lines or the size of points.
  • Asking for the size of a level of a texture.
  • Giving pixels to a texture that it renders to, and copying part of the framebuffer to a texture that it gave pixels.

Comfort

With measure_depth in the comfort of the recipe, GLEyeRenderer reads the depth buffer of the left eye before a pass is cleared and at the end of the frame.

Measuring

The read is of a copy of the depth buffer that the graphics card makes small. DepthRangeMeasurer turns the depths into distances with the projection of the pass. The nearest and farthest surfaces are the edges of what was drawn. What was not drawn is the sky, which counts as being at the far plane when it covers enough of the picture.

FastDepthRangeMeasurer gives the same numbers as DepthRangeMeasurer::measure() to the last bit.

  • On a processor with AVX2 (found out at its start; DepthHistogramAvx2 in libs/stereo/core) it counts the distances in a histogram, the bins being the top bits of the distance as a float, with the pixels taken eight at a time. It picks the three distances among those of three bins only.
  • Without AVX2 the scalar measure is used.

Lag

  • The read is made to a buffer of the graphics card, which the converter does not wait for: the result is taken at the end of the frame after it.
  • The depth that is given is that of the frame before, with the scene key of that frame (GLEyeRenderer::get_depth_lag()).
  • A frame with no depth in it is left to what was baked for its shape.

Convergence by time

The nearest and farthest depth of a frame go to RecipeStereoPlanner::on_frame_depth(), which has the eyes converge and the separation limited as RecipeApplier::get_comfort_limits() says (the function that bakes the limits of a scene). The eyes then follow those values over the frames. Two comfort options of the recipe set how; both are off by default.

Attached draws

The decision is RecipeStereoPlanner::is_last_draw_attached_to_camera(). A draw whose origin is nearer the camera than camera_attached_depth of the convergence distance is made as it is for both eyes, unless one of these holds:

  • the recipe names it in eye_space_cover_keys;
  • the recipe has comfort.automatic_layers and the baker found it to be in eye space (its model-view is the identity because the application put its vertices in the space of the eye, so the origin says nothing of where they are).

The frame channel

Shared memory between the 32-bit program and the 64-bit presenter, named from the session (Local\APIStereo_<session>).

  • Images start at multiples of 64 KB and rows at multiples of 256 bytes, so the GPU can import the memory as a heap.
  • The two sides say how they can pass images (a mask of transport kinds), and the producer uses the best that both can; host memory is the one kind there is.
  • The frames are opaque: every exporter sets the alpha of the pixels to 255 when it copies a frame in. What an application draws into the alpha of its window is never shown by the system, which shows a window as opaque, and the presenter blends what it is given by alpha, so a fade that an application draws with blending would otherwise show as black.
  • The floats of the controls are stored as bits next to a has-value flag (read_float / write_float), so a reader never takes a value that was not set.

Three slots, nobody waits

There are three image slots: the producer writes one that is neither the newest nor the one being read, so nobody waits.

The log and the switches

The log is off unless asked for: --log on the launcher and on the presenter, which the VR player passes on. It is the file %TEMP%\apistereo_<session>.log. Off, only what has to be said is written: why the converter stops the application, a frame that cannot be passed on, a headset that cannot be used.

Reading the log

  • If the program's presents are even and the repeats are many, the delay is ours.
  • Seconds in which the program itself stalls (a scene that loads) show as fewer presents and many repeats, and nothing downstream can show images the program has not made.
  • With --log --wait the launcher reads the last statistics line when the application exits and says what the run came to. It reports:
    • the frames seen, and how many draws were affected, unaffected and skipped;
    • a line for each thing the converter could not do: draws of a signature the recipe does not know, frames of a scene it has no distance for, surfaces that could not be given a twin, calls not made for the right eye.

The switches

A run without the VR player takes its switches from the environment. A number is read from the variable; a flag is on when the variable exists, whatever its value is.

APISTEREO_EYE_SEPARATIONThe separation of the eyes, in the units of the program's matrices.

Holding to a rate, in short: an application whose music runs on the clock should behave as it does when a screen shows its frames, not as in a run of a thousand frames a second. Every API is held the same way (HostMemoryExporterBase::pace): by the frame_pacing of the recipe, 60 frames a second unless it says otherwise, and never when a screen keeps time.

The launcher sets APISTEREO_SESSION, APISTEREO_RECIPE and APISTEREO_LOG for the application.

A program that never exits by itself (one that loops its credits, say) must be ended by the user or the launcher; a test script that starts one must always clean up.
