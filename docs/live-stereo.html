Live stereo

The converter lives inside the program it converts. It watches each call, decides how a draw is treated, renders the second eye, and hands both images to another process.

Inside the program

Code map

The converter is made of an adapter, which knows the API of the application, and of decisions that do not. Direct3D 8 was the first API that had an adapter and Direct3D 7 is the second (below); another API gets its own, next to these, and keeps everything else. The adapter for Direct3D 8 is the files of d3d8/ in the folder of the converter (D3D8EyeRenderer, D3D8ResolutionScaler, D3D8WindowedMode and D3D8HostMemoryExporter), with Converter's callbacks and CallFeeder (and its lists of the calls it follows and of the draw calls); the one for Direct3D 7 is the files of d3d7/ (D3D7EyeRenderer, D3D7SurfaceTwins, D3D7WindowedMode and D3D7HostMemoryExporter). What the two use together (FramePasser, SessionLog, InvisibleWindows, the base of the exporters) is in the folder itself. StereoCore, ConvergenceModel, MatrixSlots, the frame channel, the presenter and the VR player have no API in them: an adapter tells StereoCore what the application does (it sets a matrix, it draws) and does what it answers.

ClassJob
ConverterRegisters the callbacks and orders the work of each call: feed the analysis, keep matrices, classify and render draws, take the settings the VR player changed.
CallFeederGives the analysis each call as a dump would; keeps the calls that wait for results in per-thread pools.
StereoCoreThe decisions: draw class from the recipe, the view matrix, unknown draws and frames. Owns the two below.
ConvergenceModelWhere the eyes converge and how far apart they are for the current draw: the VR player's settings, the eased distance of the scene, the recipe's distance of the draw.
MatrixSlotsThe matrices a rule is about and what the device has of each; says which to put in before a draw.
FramePasserPasses the presented frame on to the frame channel (at most one in 8 ms), and after the present waits for the headset to want the next one (see Keeping the frames even).
SessionLogOne line per event, in the session log that the launcher and the VR player read.

A frame

  1. The program sets matrices and draws. The hook gives each call to the feeder. A draw is analysed immediately (its signature is needed before it runs); other calls are kept until their results are in.
  2. The core looks the draw up in the recipe. A scene draw is made for the left eye, then again for the right with the eye's matrices; an interface draw is made once; a skipped one is not made.
  3. The separation of the eyes is strength × IPD scale × 1/30 × effective convergence distance, but no more than the limit of the scene (comfort in the recipe), which no strength lifts: a strength below 1 brings the limit down with it (min(strength × natural, limit × min(strength, 1))). The strength is the VR player's Depth choice: Reduced (0.65), Natural (1, the default) or More (1.25), which can be changed while the program plays; the distance comes from the recipe and is smoothed between scenes (in the log domain). The IPD scale is how far apart the eyes of the person are, against the 64 mm that the 1/30 is meant for: the presenter takes the distance between the two views that OpenXR gives each frame (smoothed), divides it by 64 mm, keeps it between 0.7 and 1.4 and says it in the frame channel when it changes by half a percent (FrameChannel::set_ipd_scale()), so adjusting the IPD of the headset while wearing it changes the depth within a second, and the far parts of a scene stay the same part of the distance between a person's eyes. The limit of the scene is not scaled: it is a limit of what the eyes bear at the nearest surface.
  4. At the end of the frame the images are passed on to the frame channel: the GPU copies them to system memory surfaces that the exporter keeps, and a thread of the exporter copies the rows to the channel while the program renders the next frame (two sets of surfaces; the program waits for the thread only when it has the next frame to hand over).

Resolution

A program like fr08 asks for a back buffer of 800 × 600, which is a small picture in a headset. The converter therefore renders it larger, at the resolution of the headset. The presenter asks the OpenXR runtime for the size of the image of an eye that it recommends (xrEnumerateViewConfigurationViews, recommendedImageRectWidth and Height; 2064 × 2208 on a Quest 3 through Link) and VR Player puts it in the frame channel before the program starts (FrameChannel::set_target_eye_size()). When the program makes its device, D3D8ResolutionScaler works out the scale from the size the program asked for (ResolutionPolicy::get_fit_scale()): the largest that fits the eye with the program's own proportions, the least of what the width and the height allow, so nothing is stretched. 800 × 600 in an eye of 2064 × 2208 is a scale of 2.58, a back buffer of 2064 × 1548. The back buffer and every render target are made that many times larger; the scale is never below 1 or above 4, and what is passed on has to fit the channel.

The only setting is Image quality in VR Player: Full (the resolution of the headset), Half of the pixels and A quarter of the pixels, which bring the target down by the square root of that in each direction, for a graphics card that is hard pressed. Without a headset, nothing is made larger. The analysis must see what the program asked for, not what was scaled, so the scaler restores the original values in the data the feeder sees (CallFeeder::set_chunk_func).

Keeping the frames even and the lag short

In a headset two things are felt: how late a picture is, and whether the motion is even. A frame that is shown twice, with the one after it skipped, is judder, and it is felt even when every frame is fast. The converter and the presenter therefore do three things: they get a frame from the program to the headset with as little work on the program's own thread as they can, they make the program produce one frame for each frame of the headset, and they hold the headset's frame for an image that is a few milliseconds late. This section says what each does, and why; the session log (see Reading the log) shows how each behaves.

The way of a frame

Each frame passes four threads. None waits for the one after it, except that the program waits for the exporter's thread when it has its next frame to hand over (and then it has been through long ago).

What was changed, and why

The first version rendered the program at four times its size for each eye. The copy out of the program held the program back, and the headset showed many of its frames a second time, though the compositor itself was never the problem. The steps, in the order they were taken:

One frame for each frame of the headset

A program that renders as fast as it can, and a headset that shows frames at a fixed rate, drift against each other. The top half of the figure shows it with a program that is a little faster: a frame of the program is made that the compositor never takes (image 1), and with a slower program a frame is taken twice. It is felt as judder even when nothing is slow, and it needs no hitch to happen.

With the headset, the presenter therefore tells the program when to make a frame. Each time the compositor asks for one (xrWaitFrame returns) the presenter signals the frame channel (FrameChannel::signal_pace(), a named event). The converter, after the program has presented a frame, waits for that signal (wait_for_pace()), and only then lets the program begin the next one. So the program makes one frame for each frame of the headset, and begins it when the headset asks, which also leaves the GPU room.

  • The delay. The frame is not begun at the signal but a delay after it, so that its images are ready at the right moment (see below). The delay is counted from the signal and not from the moment the program notices it: a frame that has to wait for the signal is then not longer by the delay, and one that is late goes on at once. (When the delay was added to the wait, the program alternated between long and short frames.) The wait is made with a timer that is fine to a millisecond or less, since a coarser one would be a frame.
  • The right moment. The compositor builds its layers as soon as it asks for a frame, from the images that are ready by then. The presenter therefore measures how far into a frame of the headset each set of images is ready (HeadsetSink::tune_pace()) and moves the delay until it is ready at 0.3 of a frame. The copy takes more than a frame, so this cannot be worked out beforehand; the log shows it as images ready into the frame and pace delay.
  • Where it cannot hurt. A program is not made to wait for a presenter that has not signalled for a quarter of a second (a headset that was taken off), nor longer than 100 ms for one signal. The delay and the signal are in the header of the channel.

An image that is a few milliseconds late

The copy of a frame to the headset takes a good part of a frame, and varies, so the images are sometimes ready just after the compositor asks. Without help, that frame shows the one before again, and the next shows the new images a frame late: a repeat and a skip for one late copy. The room is there to avoid it: the compositor's frame can be ended any time within its period, and it is ended in under a millisecond.

How long it takes, and what it does not change

From the start of the program's frame to the compositor taking its images there is the work of the four threads of the figure, and a margin before the next request: about two frames of the headset. That is how far the picture lags behind what the program did. The program's own sound is not delayed, so the picture trails the music by that much. The margin is on purpose; a smaller one gives a shorter lag and more repeats when a copy is late (the target of 0.3 of a frame can be changed in HeadsetSink::tune_pace()).

The headset's own pipeline (the runtime, the link to the headset) comes after this and is not measured here. The screen is fixed in the room, and the compositor moves it with the head by itself, so the lag does not lag the head: only the picture on the screen.

Reading the log

The log is off unless it is asked for: --log on StereoLauncher and StereoPresenter (VR Player has no control for it). (Off, only what has to be said is written: why the converter stops the application, a frame that cannot be passed on, and a headset that cannot be used.) %TEMP%\apistereo_<session>.log has these lines each second, as count, mean, worst and how many were late, in milliseconds. Seconds in which the program stalls (a scene that loads, a gap of 100 ms or more) show as fewer presents and many repeats. The converter and the presenter cannot show images the program has not made, and the log tells such a second from a delay of ours: if the program's presents are even and the repeats are many, the delay is ours.

The switches

  • Pacing the application to the headset. VR Player always does it. The presenter takes --no-pacing to leave it off, to see the difference, or if a program does not agree with being held (one whose timing depends on how fast it presents).
  • No preview with the headset. The channel has room for one reader, and the preview's copy of each image cost the same memory bandwidth as the headset's. The window says so; without a headset it previews as before.
  • The resolution (above) is the headset's own, and the copies were made fast enough for it, not by making the picture smaller. A higher resolution makes every stage above longer in proportion; Image quality is there for a graphics card that cannot keep up.

What is left

  • The program's own stalls. A scene that loads, or generates content, can take several frames of the headset in one; the headset then shows the image before. Nothing downstream can fix it.
  • The GPU copy out of the program is the largest part of the frame the program is held for. It waits for the GPU to finish rendering, which D3D8 cannot be asked to do later.
  • The copy to the headset's images waits for the GPU on the CPU, and has spikes. The OpenXR binding for Direct3D 12 has the runtime wait for the work that was submitted to the session's queue, so the wait is probably only needed to keep the shared memory safe, and could be given up for a fence that is looked at later.
  • Sharing the GPU's own surfaces with the presenter instead of the system memory would take the CPU copies away; Direct3D 8 cannot do it, so it would take a wrapper for the program's device.

Direct3D 7

A program of Direct3D 7 whose vertices are in pixels (Kasparov draws only those: a position in pixels and the reciprocal of the depth, which no matrix moves) is converted by the same pieces, with a different renderer. The same CallFeeder follows its calls through the analysis, and StereoCore decides each draw from the recipe; what is done to the vertices is decided by ScreenParallaxPlanner, which the replay uses too, and the bytes are moved by Stereo::D3D7::ScreenParallax (a vertex is moved by its depth, so that what is at the distance of convergence stays where it is).

  • The second eye. D3D7SurfaceTwins makes a copy of every surface the program renders to (render targets, depth buffers, its back buffer) as the program makes them. For each draw or clear D3D7EyeRenderer puts the twins in the device (the render target, the textures the program set), draws the right eye with the vertices moved one way, puts back what the program set, and draws the left eye with them moved the other way, itself, since the vertices have to be a copy. The copies that the program makes to a surface which has a twin (a clear with Blt) are made to the twin as well. A draw from a vertex buffer is read out of the buffer (it is locked to be read), and is then a draw of vertices in memory, of the format of the buffer, which is moved like the others; a strided draw is made for the right eye as it is, with the arrays that the program gave (they are of vertices that a camera moves, and no matrix is known here). What it cannot repeat (a vertex buffer that cannot be locked to be read, one that the runtime has optimized) is counted and told in the log.
  • The window. When another program shows the images (VR Player says how large they are before the program starts), D3D7WindowedMode has the program render to a window that is invisible: the cooperative level is that of a window, the display mode is not set, the surface that is shown has no chain of buffers, and the back buffer that the program gets from it is a surface of the converter's own, of the size of the display mode times the render scale. The hooks cannot change an argument of Direct3D 7 in place, so the call is not made and the converter makes its own, and writes the result where the program is to get it. A flip shows nothing. Without a program that shows the images, the program is on the screen as it is.
  • The resolution. The back buffer, its twin and the depth buffer of the program are made as large as the images are shown (the policy is that of Direct3D 8, see above). The viewport, the rectangles of a clear and the positions of the vertices are scaled to match; a copy or a lock of the back buffer in the program's own pixels cannot be, and is counted. One case is made right: a program which locks the whole back buffer, writes it and unlocks it (the ending screens of Kasparov are made so: a picture of 640 x 480 each frame) has what it wrote in the corner of the larger back buffer, so after the unlock it is stretched to the whole of it and copied to the twin, and both eyes show it (it is flat). A lock to read, or of part of the surface, is only counted.
  • The images. The back buffer is read before a flip, or before the blit of the surface the program rendered to onto the primary surface, which is how a program in a window shows a frame (locked, converted to B, G, R of 8 bits) into the memory of the channel, the left eye and the right side by side. With no program that shows the images, the program is held to 60 frames a second by the converter itself, since there is no flip to wait for.

With --log --wait, StereoLauncher reads the last of those statistics when the application exits and says what the run came to: the frames converted, and a line for each thing the converter could not do (draws of a signature the recipe does not know, frames of a scene it has no distance for, surfaces that could not be given a twin, calls that were not made for the right eye).

The log has, with the statistics every 600 frames, how many surfaces have a twin, how many twins could not be made, how many calls were not made for the right eye (the first of them are named), and how long the callbacks of the converter took in a frame. A run without VR Player has these switches in the environment: APISTEREO_EYE_SEPARATION, APISTEREO_DEPTH_STRENGTH (1 is the natural 3D, and the one that has the separation follow the limit of each scene), APISTEREO_PUSH_OVERLAY, APISTEREO_TARGET_EYE_WIDTH and APISTEREO_TARGET_EYE_HEIGHT (which also have the program render to the invisible window), APISTEREO_DUMP_FRAME and APISTEREO_DUMP_EVERY (a frame, both eyes, to %TEMP%\apistereo_frame_N.bmp), and, to measure, APISTEREO_NO_RIGHT_EYE and APISTEREO_NO_FRAME_LIMIT.

What the live converter does not know is which pass a draw is in, so a recipe that needs to tell the draws of one signature apart by their pass (an interface that shares its signature with what is a scene elsewhere) is refused by RecipeBake. Kasparov has no interface to push.

Frame channel

Shared memory between the 32-bit program and the 64-bit presenter. Three image slots: the producer writes one that is neither the newest nor the one being read, so nobody waits. Images start at multiples of 64 KB and rows at multiples of 256 bytes, so the GPU can import the memory as a heap.

A program that never exits by itself (the demos loop their credits) must be ended by the user or the launcher; test scripts that start one must always clean up.
